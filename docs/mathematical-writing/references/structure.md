# Section and manuscript structure

Identify the section's or paper's mathematical purpose and intended reader from available context. Organize exposition around the dependency structure and meaningful milestones, not the order results were discovered.

Put a useful result or intelligible model within reach early. A technical main theorem may need a clearly labeled informal version or special case before its precise statement. Formal dependencies must be available when used, but an explicitly deferred proof is legitimate.

Begin a substantial section with a short purpose or objective when its role is not already obvious. Give descriptive titles and transitions that say why the next argument is needed. Do not add ceremonial introductions to tiny subsections.

Keep related statements, proofs, and applications near one another where practical. Move a lengthy necessary computation or a change of technical subject to an appendix only if doing so improves the main argument's flow. Preserve enough explanation of its output for the body to remain intelligible.

A toy example can expose the essential mechanism before generality. Include it when it reduces conceptual load rather than repeating an already transparent argument.

For major reorganization, work out a compact dependency outline before rewriting. Preserve labels and external references where possible. Avoid proving the same argument twice; isolate a common argument when that is mathematically natural, not by forcing an obscure generalization.

Respect the chosen scope. A section rewrite may expose a larger organizational problem; report it briefly rather than rewriting unrelated sections without authorization.
