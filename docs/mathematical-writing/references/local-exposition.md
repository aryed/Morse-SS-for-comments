# Local exposition

## Statements and definitions

State usable hypotheses and conclusions, with unambiguous quantifier order and notation. Keep a milestone understandable without making readers reconstruct its assumptions from distant prose; use a precise local reference when repeating the full setup would obscure it.

Introduce new terminology when it captures a reusable idea or materially simplifies the argument. Avoid naming a one-use conjunction of technical conditions. Use an example or non-example when it clarifies a genuinely unfamiliar definition; familiar definitions and short snippets need no ritual example. An example before an abstract definition can provide a mental model; an example after it can test the definition. Choose by the reader's needs.

For a requested example, explain what it demonstrates, specify its data, and show the decisive calculation or verification. State what it does not establish when that limitation is material to the claim.

## Proofs

For a long or conceptually difficult proof, give the strategy before details. For a short direct argument, begin the argument. Present the logical route rather than discovery history.

Spend detail on the new step, an obscure convention, or the point where a hypothesis matters. For machinery central to the proof, identify the applicable result and check its relevant hypotheses. Do not hide a missing transition behind “standard” or “clear”; do not expand every familiar fact into a separate lemma either.

Extract a lemma when it packages a meaningful reusable milestone or hides notation that the reader can then forget. Merge intermediate lemmas used only to reach one conclusion when this improves understanding. Keep independently useful statements visible.

Translate cited results into current notation where necessary. Distinguish a cited theorem, a proved adaptation, and an unverified expectation. A constructive existence proof may read best as a construction followed by its properties.

## Prose and notation

Use English to express implication, contrast, dependence, and generalization. Avoid long symbol chains when their logical grouping is unclear. Distinguish “such that” from a claimed consequence.

Let notation emphasize important parameters. Prefer descriptors such as “the index bundle V” when they help readers remember a symbol's role. Avoid introducing a symbol merely to replace a short phrase used once.

Display formulas when they need visual space, comparison, or alignment; punctuate them as part of sentences. Use no fixed character threshold. Preserve the existing reference, numbering, and macro system; suggest global changes separately if warranted.
