# Titles, abstracts, and introductions

Ground the contribution in the actual statements and their verified status. If the manuscript or results are unavailable, use only supplied facts and clearly identify missing information; do not invent novelty or literature comparisons.

## Abstract and title

Make the title informative about the mathematical object and contribution. Make the abstract a short, self-contained account of the main result and essential scope. For a single-result paper, a readable statement may suffice. Include a method only when it meaningfully distinguishes the work. Retain hypotheses necessary to avoid misleading claims.

Avoid unexplained notation, an internal roadmap, citations needed to decode the result, or unsupported superlatives. Do not announce an obstruction, equivalence, or application that the paper only conjectures.

## Introduction

Give the target reader enough shared context to see the question, identify what is missing, state what the paper establishes, and explain its significance. Present the main results early enough to orient the reader. Use precise statements when accessible; otherwise label an informal description or special case and point to the exact statement.

Explain how the result or method differs from the relevant prior work using grounded comparisons. Curiosity is valid motivation: do not invent a fashionable application or exaggerate progress toward a famous problem.

Give the central idea or proof strategy at a useful level. Separate rigorous assertions from motivation and heuristic pictures. State important limitations or open questions candidly.

Add a roadmap only if it helps navigation. Fit organization to the actual paper; do not force a fixed introduction recipe, a conclusion, or an abstract into a task that excludes them.
