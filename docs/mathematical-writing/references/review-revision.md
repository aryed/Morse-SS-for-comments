# Review and revision

Infer whether the request is editorial critique, mathematical checking, or both. Keep those assessments distinct. Do not present a writing review as certification of correctness.

For a whole paper, first understand the main claim, audience, and dependency outline; then assess whether the title, abstract, introduction, and body tell the same accurate story. For a section or proof, stay at that scope.

Prioritize issues by their effect:
- A false or overstated claim, missing hypothesis, unresolved dependency, or suspected proof gap.
- A structural obstacle that hides the result or makes the argument hard to follow.
- A local explanation, notation, or prose problem.
- Optional cosmetic preferences only when requested or materially useful.

For each substantive finding, give a location, the specific problem, its consequence for the reader or mathematics, and a concrete repair. Label suspected issues and uncertainty; do not manufacture criticism or report unsupported claims of completeness.

For proof simplification, identify the proposed replacement and verify that it preserves hypotheses and conclusion. If verification is incomplete, explain the promising step and the remaining gap without replacing the proof as though resolved.

When rewriting, preserve the author's voice and manuscript conventions. Briefly explain major organizational changes. A polished replacement may accompany criticism when requested, but critique alone does not authorize changing source files.

End with the smallest useful next revision step when there is unfinished work. Avoid a long checklist of minor preferences after the principal problems are clear.
